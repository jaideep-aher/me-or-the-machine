Me or the Machine

Can the people closest to me tell one sentence I said from one an AI wrote in my voice?
Jaideep Aher · AIPI 590 Responsible AI · Duke University · Fall 2026 · Final project report
Abstract. AI assistants and always-on voice wearables now hold enough of a person's speech to imitate them. I ran a blind discrimination test: six people who know me (a partner and five friends) judged 140 single lines, half taken from my own wearable recordings and half written by a language model told to sound like me. They were right on 70 of 140 judgments (50.0%, 95% Wilson interval 41.8% to 58.2%, exact binomial p = 1.00 against chance). My real lines and the twin's lines were each judged "really him" 41 times out of 70. Judges leaned towards trust (59% "really him"), which favours an impostor, and three judges who saw the same 20 lines agreed on only 5; their majority vote did no better than one judge. For short text, "my friends would know it wasn't me" did not hold in this pilot.

1. Goal and threat model

Goal. Measure whether close contacts can act as a human safeguard against text impersonation built from a person's own recorded speech, and make the risk concrete enough for a non-technical audience to act on.

Why now. Recording pendants have moved from niche to mainstream. Plaud reports more than 2 million devices sold and passed 1 million units by July 2025; Amazon bought the Bee pendant (used for this project) in July 2025 and Meta bought Limitless in December 2025. Meanwhile the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report counted more than 22,000 complaints involving AI-enabled scams with about $893 million in reported losses, and the FTC has warned since 2023 about scammers impersonating distressed family members with cloned voices. (A widely repeated "7,000% year over year" growth figure for voice pendants could not be traced to any source and is not used here.)

2. Method

2.1 Real lines

Source: my own Bee wearable export (speech-to-text). I kept only utterances labelled as me, that stand alone without context, and removed every conversation about relationships, health, family or money, plus any line naming another person. Other people's speech was never used. 48 real lines were selected. They keep their transcription errors.

2.2 Twin lines

47 lines were written by Claude (Opus) in a chat session after it had read the real lines, asked to match my vocabulary, fillers ("like", "you know what I mean"), topics (AI tools, classes, job hunting, food) and sentence length. This is a strong, data-informed twin, not a deployed system.

2.3 Test

A web page shows one shuffled line at a time with two buttons: "really him" or "AI twin". No feedback appears until the end, so judges cannot learn my style mid-round. Four line sets were used: A (30 lines), B (20), C (30) and D, a fixed set of 20 balanced lines that several friends took on their own phones so that per-line agreement could be measured. Results save automatically (claude.ai artifact store for A to C, a Supabase insert-only table for D).

2.4 Participants and ethics

Six judges: P1 (partner, third attempt on set A) and P2 to P6 (friends). Every judge was told in advance that some lines were AI-written. No message was ever sent to anyone as me. Names are replaced by P1 to P6 in the repository.

2.5 Analysis

Accuracy against 50% with a two-sided exact binomial test and Wilson 95% intervals; the rate at which real and twin lines were called "really him"; overall "really him" response rate (trust bias); per-line unanimity and majority vote for the three set-D judges; tech versus everyday topic. Script: analysis/analyze.py (standard library only).

  • Chance-level accuracy. 70 of 140, 95% interval 41.8% to 58.2%, p = 1.00. Only P2 (14 of 20) was above chance, and a single 70% score on 20 lines is not significant on its own (p ≈ 0.12).
  • No separation. The label of a line (real or twin) did not change how often it was called "him": 41 of 70 in both groups.
  • Trust bias. Judges answered "really him" 59% of the time. In a real attack, where every message claims to be from me, this bias helps the impostor.
  • Low agreement. On set D, all three judges agreed on only 5 of 20 lines: two twin lines all three believed, one of my real lines all three rejected, and two correct calls. Their majority vote was right on 10 of 20, so pooling judges did not help.
  • Topic did not matter. Lines about AI tools and gadgets were called "him" 59% of the time and everyday lines 58%.

4. Discussion

With one sentence and no context, people who know me well could not separate my speech from an imitation. Their errors were not random noise around a skill; they were the absence of a usable signal, plus a lean towards trust. The partner, who knows me best, scored exactly 50%. This matters for the threat in Figure 1: the cheapest attack is a short message, and that is exactly the format where human detection failed.

The result does not show that an AI can impersonate me in a conversation. Shared memories, follow-up questions and timing are likely to help a judge, and that is the obvious defence: a question only the real person can answer, or a call back on a known number.

5. Limitations

  • Small pilot: six judges, 140 judgments, four line sets.
  • No control twin that knows nothing about me, so the study cannot yet say how much my personal data contributed.
  • Twin lines were written with the real lines in view, a best case for the twin.
  • Real lines carry speech-to-text errors that may make them look less like me; the twin's lines are clean.
  • P1 was on a third attempt and had seen some lines before.
  • Judges knew AI lines were present, which may have made them more suspicious than a real target.

6. Responsible AI considerations

  • Dual use. The repository ships no automated twin, no prompts tuned for impersonation, and only benign one-liners.
  • Bystander consent. Wearables record other people who never agreed. None of their speech was used.
  • Recommendations. Wearable makers: minimise retention and keep transcripts on device. Platforms: label AI-written messages. Individuals: agree a family verification question; treat urgent money requests by text as unverified.

7. Future work

  1. A control twin with no personal data, to measure what the data adds.
  2. One fixed set judged by ten or more people, with a reason for each answer.
  3. Multi-turn chats, to find where shared history starts to protect people.
  4. Test the verification-question defence directly.
