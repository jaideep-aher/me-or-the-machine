I've been recorded 24/7 for 7 weeks. Now an AI can be me.

Me or the Machine: can the people closest to me tell one sentence I said from one an AI wrote in my voice?
Jaideep Aher · AIPI 590 Responsible AI · Duke University · Fall 2026 · Final project report
Abstract. AI assistants and always-on voice wearables now hold enough of a person's speech to imitate them. My own Bee pendant has recorded and transcribed nearly everything I have said since 2 October 2026, about seven weeks. I ran a blind discrimination test on lines from it: ten people who know me (a partner and nine friends) judged 250 single lines, half taken from my own wearable recordings and half written by a language model told to sound like me. On the 160 judgments with saved answers they were right 78 times (48.8%, 95% Wilson interval 41.1% to 56.4%, exact binomial p = 0.81); averaged per judge, 49% (judge-level bootstrap 43% to 56%). Adding three friends' reported 30-line scores gives 111 of 250 (44.4%). Twin lines were judged "really him" as often as my real ones (47 and 45 of 80; sensitivity d′ = −0.06). Judges leaned towards trust (58% "really him"), which favours an impostor, and four judges who saw the same 20 lines agreed unanimously on just one line, a twin line they all believed. For short text, "my friends would know it wasn't me" did not hold in this pilot.

1. Goal and threat model

Goal. Measure whether close contacts can act as a human safeguard against text impersonation built from a person's own recorded speech, and make the risk concrete enough for a non-technical audience to act on.

Why now. Recording pendants have moved from niche to mainstream. Plaud reports more than 2 million devices sold and passed 1 million units by July 2025; Amazon bought the Bee pendant (used for this project) in July 2025 and Meta bought Limitless in December 2025. Meanwhile the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report counted more than 22,000 complaints involving AI-enabled scams with about $893 million in reported losses, and the FTC has warned since 2023 about scammers impersonating distressed family members with cloned voices. (A widely repeated "7,000% year over year" growth figure for voice pendants could not be traced to any source and is not used here.)

2. Method

2.1 Real lines

Source: my own Bee wearable, worn around the clock since 2 October 2026 (about seven weeks of conversations, transcribed by the device). I kept only utterances labelled as me, that stand alone without context, and removed every conversation about relationships, health, family or money, plus any line naming another person. Other people's speech was never used. 48 real lines were selected. They keep their transcription errors.

2.2 Twin lines

47 lines were written by Claude (Opus) in a chat session after it had read the real lines, asked to match my vocabulary, fillers ("like", "you know what I mean"), topics (AI tools, classes, job hunting, food) and sentence length. This is a strong, data-informed twin, not a deployed system.

2.3 Test

A web page shows one shuffled line at a time with two buttons: "really him" or "AI twin". No feedback appears until the end, so judges cannot learn my style mid-round. Four line sets were used: A (30 lines), B (20), C (30) and D, a fixed set of 20 balanced lines that several friends took on their own phones so that per-line agreement could be measured. Results save automatically (claude.ai artifact store for A to C, a Supabase insert-only table for D).

2.4 Participants and ethics

Ten judges: P1 (partner, third attempt on set A) and P2 to P10 (friends). P7 took set D; P8 to P10 each took a 30-line round whose scores were reported to me as about 41%, 49% and 20% and are recorded as the nearest possible scores (12, 15 and 6 of 30); their per-line answers were not saved, so they count only towards overall accuracy. Every judge was told in advance that some lines were AI-written. No message was ever sent to anyone as me. Names are replaced by P1 to P10 in the repository.

2.5 Analysis

Accuracy against 50% with a two-sided exact binomial test and Wilson 95% intervals; the rate at which real and twin lines were called "really him"; overall "really him" response rate (trust bias); per-line unanimity and majority vote for the three set-D judges; tech versus everyday topic. Script: analysis/analyze.py (standard library only).

  • No better than chance. Primary result, saved answers only: 78 of 160 (48.8%, 95% interval 41.1% to 56.4%, p = 0.81). Because decisions are clustered within judges, I also tested judges as units: mean accuracy 49%, bootstrap over judges 43% to 56%, one judge above 50% and three below (sign test p = 0.63). Including three reported scores: 111 of 250 (44.4%, p = 0.09). Only P2 (14 of 20) scored above chance, not significant on its own (p ≈ 0.12).
  • No separation. Twin lines were called "him" 47 of 80 times, my real lines 45 of 80. In signal-detection terms d′ = −0.06 and the criterion c = −0.19, a lean towards answering "him".
  • One unusually low score. P10 reported 6 of 30, which would be well below chance (p = 0.001) if exact. The answers were not saved, so I treat it as unverified.
  • Trust bias. Judges answered "really him" 58% of the time. In a real attack, where every message claims to be from me, this bias helps the impostor.
  • Low agreement. On set D, four judges agreed unanimously on just 1 of 20 lines: a twin line about a biryani place near campus, which all four believed. Their majority vote was right on 3 lines, wrong on 5 and tied on 12, so pooling judges did not help.

4. Discussion

With one sentence and no context, people who know me well could not separate my speech from an imitation. Their errors were not random noise around a skill; they were the absence of a usable signal, plus a lean towards trust. The equivalence test and Bayes factor in section 3.1 show this is evidence of no ability, not only a lack of data. The partner, who knows me best, scored exactly 50%, and accuracy on saved answers (49%) sat on chance. This matters for the threat in Figure 1: the cheapest attack is a short message, and that is exactly the format where human detection failed.

The result does not show that an AI can impersonate me in a conversation. Shared memories, follow-up questions and timing are likely to help a judge, and that is the obvious defence: a question only the real person can answer, or a call back on a known number.

5. Limitations

  • Small pilot: ten judges, 250 judgments.
  • Three 30-line scores were reported verbally and rounded; their line-by-line answers were not saved.
  • No control twin that knows nothing about me, so the study cannot yet say how much my personal data contributed.
  • Twin lines were written with the real lines in view, a best case for the twin.
  • I chose real lines that stand alone without context, which may favour generic lines that are easy to imitate.
  • Real lines carry speech-to-text errors that may make them look less like me; the twin's lines are clean.
  • P1 was on a third attempt and had seen some lines before.
  • Judges knew AI lines were present, which may have made them more suspicious than a real target.

6. What an AI recorder holds, and why leaks matter

While choosing lines for this study, I found the pendant's AI summaries had picked up details of my finances and private health information. I had never thought of those conversations as being recorded. It scared me.

After seven weeks my pendant holds every conversation word for word, an AI summary of each day, where I was, other people's words recorded without their consent, and talk about health, money and relationships. This study shows one use of such a record: a convincing text twin from just 48 lines. A leak would enable more: blackmail, answers to account security questions, physical location, and exposure of everyone I talk to.

The pendant is not the only store. Before this project I built Jarvis, a personal AI assistant connected to almost everything about me: the Bee pendant, three Gmail accounts, Google Calendar, Tasks, Docs and Sheets, Canvas, phone location and app use, my Mac activity, and a page built to take my medical record. Its Memory page reports 7,273 items from 11 sources, its People page tracks 200 contacts, and it charts my distraction patterns. One such system is both very useful and a single target holding most of a life.

Leaks of intimate data keep happening. In 2023 Amazon, which now owns Bee, paid a $25 million FTC penalty for keeping children's Alexa voice recordings after parents asked for deletion, and in a separate case the FTC said Ring employees could view customers' private videos (Amazon denied wrongdoing). In 2023 attackers took data on 6.9 million 23andMe users, including genetic and health reports, undetected for about five months. In January 2025 researchers found a DeepSeek database exposing users' chat logs with no password.

Advice to anyone considering a pendant: assume the data will leak one day; mute it for private conversations and tell people when it is on; delete transcripts regularly and prefer on-device processing; use a strong unique password with two-factor login and few connected apps; and agree a family check question instead of trusting a text.

7. Responsible AI considerations

  • Dual use. The repository ships no automated twin, no prompts tuned for impersonation, and only benign one-liners.
  • Bystander consent. Wearables record other people who never agreed. None of their speech was used.
  • Recommendations. Wearable makers: minimise retention and keep transcripts on device. Platforms: label AI-written messages. Individuals: agree a family verification question; treat urgent money requests by text as unverified.

8. Next steps

  1. Control twin with no personal data. If it passes as often, one-line impersonation needs no data at all.
  2. Twin fed all seven weeks of recordings. Measures how much the pendant record itself adds to the risk.
  3. Multi-turn conversations and a cloned voice, where shared memories should help friends.
  4. Learning with feedback. The live test now reveals each answer; those rounds (set DF) show whether accuracy rises over 20 lines.
  5. Test the defence: whether a family check question stops a twin that has read weeks of my life.
  6. Audit AI recorders: what each stores, for how long and who can access it, published as a plain checklist.
